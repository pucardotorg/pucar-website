# Statement of Objects & Reasons

The High Court of [*State*] has set up new courts called the 24x7 ON Courts. The processes, technology, and governance of these new courts have been designed keeping people at the center. The core values of these courts are efficiency, accessibility, predictability, and transparency. These courts are open and networked to the surrounding institutions to enable a seamless experience for all court users.

We need new rules to equip and enable these new courts. The existing criminal rules of practice have been designed to support the existing court system. In addition, for some aspects the existing rules even limit the full potential of transforming the experience of court users.

These Criminal Rules of Practice have been designed to support the new 24x7 ON Courts. The rules are divided into three parts. A ‘Preliminary’ section that sets the intent, scope, and definitions for these rules. A ‘Case Stages’ section that the users can refer to for rules at each stage of the case. A ‘Case Progress and Management’ section that details aspects like issuing of processes, case scheduling, etc. And finally, a ‘Miscellaneous’ section that covers more detailed rules for aspects like court records and communication, court language, etc.

At present, for practical purposes, these rules cover cheque dishonour cases as the 24x7 ON Court presently deals with such cases. However, the intent is not to limit the application of these rules to such cases alone, and to over time expand these rules to cover other kinds of criminal cases as well. Therefore, the rules do not explicitly limit themselves to cheque dishonour cases, however the High Court can account for this aspect when it notifies the courts to which these Rules apply.

### What are some of the key changes that these rules introduce, and why?

1. *Optimization of judicial time for aspects that need judicial application of mind*: Judge time and bandwidth is a scarce resource. It must be spent on judicial functions. The new rules provide for a division of responsibilities - the judge is enabled to perform the actions that require judicial application of mind; whereas the court more broadly, including the court staff or the digital platform that powers the court, is enabled to perform ministerial and administrative responsibilities such as rescheduling of cases. Further, it is clarified that just because an action requires judicial application of mind, the judge is not prevented from using the digital platform for such actions, as long as the Magistrate is controlling the results.

2. *The Court can use its Digital Platform as an agent to perform administrative functions*: Aside from delegating certain non-judicial responsibilities to the court officers, the Court is also empowered to use technology to aid it in ministerial and rule-based aspects like scrutiny, listing, etc. The new rules prescribe that after the judges set up and transparently publish the standard rules for escalation of summons, rules for file scrutiny, and for scheduling of cases, the execution of these rules is a ministerial function that the court can oversee over periodic intervals. The Court can also use these systems to guide about the information that they must provide at the time of filing or responding to a case.

3. *A case can be progressed asynchronously without a hearing*: Today, a hearing is required to progress the case. A hearing requires synchronous availability and presence of the judge, parties, and their lawyers. Considerable time is spent in coordinating this schedule. However, many aspects of a case can be progressed without a hearing. They do not require synchronous presence, or even the presence of all these three court users. The new rules enable the court to take actions like reissuing summons, or rescheduling a case to a next date, etc., as soon as the need arises without the requirement of a hearing, and without necessitating synchronous presence of all these three users.

4. *Additional certified true copies of documents like orders & judgments are not required*: Before, when copies of documents were made physically on paper, you would require the court to physically stamp these documents to certify that the photocopies were accurate versions of the original. In a digitally-native court, the original version of the orders, judgments, and other information can be made available on the court system/ website, for the users to download. Additional methods of verification, like stamping by the court as true copies, would not be required for the court to consider these documents valid. This also means that parties need not pay additional copying or other fees for this purpose.

5. *Provision of clear and structured communication to the users for clarity and transparency: *For example, the new rules provide for summons to give clear guidance to parties, including information on the nature of the complaint, required documents, next steps, and timelines. Similarly, the rules provide guidance on aspects that an order must include, in order to provide clarity to the parties about next steps.

6. *Multi-channel delivery of processes*: Processes can be delivered both on physical and electronic addresses, increasing the likelihood of delivery and appearance of the accused.

7. *Additional applications for aspects that are a matter of right, like bail in a bailable offence, are not required*: Bail terms in offences like cheque dishonour cases are fairly standard, unless the case concerns special circumstances. In such cases, the judge needn’t wait for the accused to appear before the court to set bail terms, and can do so at the stage of summons itself. In this manner, within the first hearing, the accused can fulfil the bail terms or, in case of any special circumstances, request the court to modify the bail terms. In conclusion, the rules empower the judge to act on their own, without requiring the parties to initiate additional processes, thereby saving time and effort for all involved.

8. *Additional applications for aspects like refund of bail amount, etc., are not required*: Today, parties need to file an application for aspects like refund of bail amount. This is because the court operates in siloes - its various departments like accounts and courtroom do not exchange real time information with each other. In a digitally integrated, single-window court system, information can move seamlessly across internal departments, eliminating the need for formal applications to initiate processes that are purely administrative and internal to the court.

9. *Additional attestation on documents like vakalatnama or affidavit is not needed*: Today, such attestation plays the role of verifying the identity of the signatory. The Digital Platform permits such identity verification at the time of creation of the user account itself, removing the need for any additional attestation or document witnesses.

10. *Multiple signatures on different documents submitted together not needed:* Today, parties and advocates sign the complaint, affidavit, and vakalatnama separately. In a digital world, since these documents are submitted at one go, parties can affix a single sign on these documents to authenticate them. A separate signature for each of them does not add value.

11. *The Court can sit beyond court hours at the request of the parties*: In case the parties have to miss work to attend court, the rules allow them to request that their hearing be scheduled outside court hours.

12. *Promote expeditious disposal of cases*: The judge is empowered to decide as many aspects of the case as possible in a single hearing. For instance, the judge can grant bail, record the plea, and prepare a trial schedule on the same day. Similarly, the parties, their lawyers, and the judge can align on a trial schedule at the beginning of the case itself.

13. *Same case number for the case from start to end, and interim applications do not take a separate case number*: Earlier, giving interim applications distinct numbers may have assisted courts in identifying and listing them separately from the main matter. However, in a digital court system, such differentiation can be effectively managed through case metadata, making separate case numbering unnecessary. Multiple case numbers for aspects of the same matter make it difficult for the litigants and other users to track their cases. In addition, it may also lead to data inaccuracies, as one case can be recorded and counted more than once.

14. *The court provides a minimum set of services to its users including a help desk, and a feedback system: *This ensures that court users don’t feel lost in court processes and have support. This also enables the court to continuously improve itself on the basis of feedback from users.

15. *Proactive updates from the court*: Wherever possible, the court must provide proactive updates on case status, actions that the parties need to take, and fees that need to be paid, to the users.

16. *Enable an ecosystem of actors*: Rather than focus on developing all the solutions itself, the Court curates the right environment and infrastructure for solutions to emerge rapidly from the ecosystem to create a multiplier effect for change. For example, in a phased manner, the Court can enable third-party providers to integrate web-based or chat-based filing interfaces with the court filing system. Such an approach allows the judiciary to optimize its time and resources towards core judicial activities and minimise its efforts towards administrative and technology efforts, such as improving solution interfaces with users.

17. *Transparency, and accountability using open data*: The data collected in the system, as far as possible, is available on a public dashboard. This data is periodically reviewed by the Chief Judicial Magistrate, the Principal District Judge, and the High Court, to track the progress of cases, and improve the system.

18. *Maintenance of records digitally, without the need to maintain multiple registers with overlapping data*: Traditionally, courts relied on separate registers for fines, witnesses, and other administrative aspects, because physical record-keeping was the only means of ensuring accountability and institutional tracking. Much of the information recorded in these registers duplicates data already contained in the case file. In a digital system, such data can be automatically captured from electronic case records, organised in structured formats, and made accessible to relevant stakeholders through integrated dashboards, thereby reducing redundancy and improving accuracy.

19. *Eliminate process fees*: Today, process fees are charged every time the judge decides to issue or reissue the process. As per data, 30% cases at summons stages stay pending because of non-payment of this process fees. These rules do away with any additional process fees that the court charges for the issuance of processes. It also enables the court to charge any delivery fee that may be required for the processes at the time of filing itself, reducing the need to pay such delivery fees at multiple stages.

20. *Eliminate court payments using physical stamps*: Before, physical stamps were invented as a practical and secure way to verify and record payments to governments and institutions. Today, when users can make payments online, with a non-repudiable online record of transfer, the need to purchase, affix, and cancel physical stamps adds friction to the court process and is unnecessary.

21. *Eliminate Roll Call*: Today, courts in a day hear both (a) substantive aspects like evidence that required a hearing, and (b) other aspects like reissuance of summons that may not necessarily require a hearing. In addition, many cases would get adjourned. Therefore, the court would do a roll call in the morning to close the cases that are going to get adjourned and hear the cases that do not have a substantive aspect. After closing a majority of the cases like this, the court would take up hearings with substantive aspects at the end. In a people-centric court, where adjournments are not the norm, and the court can take up non-substantive matters like the reissuance of summons asynchronously, a roll call serves no purpose.

### Aside from the substantive intent of supporting the transformation of courts keeping people at the center, what are the principles that formed the basis of the drafting process of these rules?

1. **Freedom**

  Rules should be minimal. The benefit of any rule must clearly outweigh the cost of compliance.

2. **Proportionality **

  Rules must be proportionate to their objective. Rules must refrain from using high-pressure incentives or disincentives.

3. **Safety **

  Freedom ends where harm begins. Rules exist to protect people and communities from preventable harm. Unintended consequences of the Rules must be pre-empted to the extent possible. There should be clear rules about the exercise of power to prevent misuse.

4. **Empowerment **

  Rules should increase people’s capacity to act, not diminish it. They should open doors, widen choices, and strengthen individuals and communities to shape their own futures. The Rules must empower citizens to act on their own where they want, without necessarily requiring intermediation. Rules should trust the citizens, and not start from a place of mistrust.

5. **Resilience**

  Empowerment comes from adaptability. Rules should allow for multiple pathways, diverse solutions, and creative responses to change. Rules must also enable role-optionality, and, where possible, allow roles to be performed by those other than the judge or the court. Rules must be principle-based (as against detailed rule-based), enabling flexibility to the Judge to apply the principle in various scenarios.

6. **Access**

  Everyone should be able to participate and benefit.

7. **Predictability **

  Rules must enable clarity and a degree of predictability to users about the court process. Where clarity is possible ahead of time, it must be given, discretion must be reserved for aspects where it is necessary.

8. **Viability **

  Systems must endure over time. Rules should be simple, sustainable, and cost-effective, ensuring people can actually work with them. Rules should work at scale.

9. **Evolution**

  No system of rules is perfect. To remain empowering, rules must evolve.

  Rules must be technology-agnostic, not tied to a specific or existing technology.

  Systems should proactively emit transparent, analysable data, allowing citizens and policymakers to refine and improve them over time.

  Evolution is best encouraged by creating space for experimentation, where new approaches can be tested safely, and lessons from both successes and failures can feed back into the system.
